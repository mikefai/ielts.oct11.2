Reading Strategy

1. The skim–scan–read sequence

  1. Skim the passage first (60–90 seconds): read the title, first and last sentence of each paragraph. You're building a map of where topics live, not understanding every detail.
  2. Scan for the exact wording or an obvious paraphrase of each question's keywords, using your map to go straight to the right paragraph instead of re-reading from the top.
  3. Read closely only the sentence (or two) around the match you found, to confirm the answer and rule out a near-miss distractor.

2. Time management

You get one 60-minute clock for all three passages — there's no separate time per passage in the real test. As a guide: roughly 17 minutes for Passage 1, 20 minutes for Passage 2, 23 minutes for Passage 3 (passages get harder, so later ones deserve more time). This split is a coaching heuristic, not an official rule — adjust it to your own pace in practice.

Never leave a blank answer. There's no penalty for a wrong guess, so always put something down, even a guess, before time runs out.

3. All 11 question types

True / False / Not Given (TFNG)

Tests whether a statement matches a fact in the passage. True = passage confirms it. False = passage directly contradicts it. Not Given = the passage simply never mentions it — it is neither confirmed nor denied.

Passage says: "The bridge, completed in 1932, was the longest in the country for over fifty years."
Statement: "The bridge was the longest in the world." → Not Given — the passage only claims "in the country", not "in the world". It doesn't deny the world claim; it just never raises it.

Yes / No / Not Given (YNNG)

Same logic as TFNG, but tests the writer's opinion or claim, not a fact. Used in passages that argue a position.

Multiple Choice (MCQ)

Read the question stem first, predict an answer in your own words, then eliminate options that are true but not what was asked, too extreme, or correct for the wrong part of the passage.

Matching Headings (MH)

You get more headings than paragraphs — some are decoys. Match the paragraph's main idea, not a single word that happens to appear in it; the decoys are usually built from a repeated keyword attached to the wrong idea.

Matching Information (MI)

You're told which piece of information to find (an example, a date, a reason, a comparison) and must say which paragraph it's in. Scan for the type of information requested, not just a keyword.

Matching Features (MF)

Match a name, date, or term to a description. Watch for distractors where the same person/thing is mentioned twice but only one mention matches the specific claim in the question.

Sentence Completion (SC)

Complete a sentence using words taken directly from the passage, inside a stated word limit (e.g. "NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS"). Check grammar: your answer must fit the sentence that's already started.

Summary Completion (SumC)

Like sentence completion, but across a short paragraph-length summary of part of the passage. Read the whole summary first to know the topic, then fill gaps in order.

Note / Table / Flow-Chart / Diagram Completion

Same word-limit rules as above, applied to a structured format. Diagram labelling often tests vocabulary for parts of a process or object — scan for the relevant stage, not the whole passage.

Short Answer (SA)

Answer a direct question using words from the passage, inside a word limit. Answer exactly what's asked — no extra words, or the word-limit rule marks it wrong even if the idea is right.

Matching Sentence Endings (MSE)

A sentence beginning must be completed with the logically and grammatically correct ending from a list. Several endings may seem plausible — only one fits both the grammar and the passage's meaning.

Turkish L1 pattern: "Not Given" read as "False" Turkish academic writing tends to state things explicitly, so Turkish-speaking readers often assume that if an idea isn't stated, it must be false. Ask yourself: does the passage contradict this (False), or does it simply never mention it (Not Given)? If you can't point to the sentence that denies it, it's Not Given.
Turkish L1 pattern: word-for-word translation of collocations When scanning for a paraphrase, Turkish speakers sometimes expect the exact Turkish-collocation equivalent (e.g. looking for "make a decision" when the passage paraphrases with "settled on") and miss the match. Train yourself to expect synonyms and restructured sentences, not just substituted single words.

Writing Task 1 Strategy

20 minutes · 150 words minimum · describe, don't explain causes or give opinions.

1. The three-part structure

  1. Introduction (1 sentence) — paraphrase the task statement in your own words.
  2. Overview (1–2 sentences) — the two or three biggest patterns, with no numbers. This is the single highest-value sentence in the whole task.
  3. Body paragraphs (2 paragraphs) — selected supporting data, grouped logically (by category, by time period, or by similarity/difference), not every number in the chart.

2. The overview formula

"Overall, it is clear that [biggest trend/comparison #1], while [biggest trend/comparison #2]."

Write the overview before you look at small details — if you can't summarise the chart in one sentence without numbers, you haven't understood its main story yet.

3. Data selection

You cannot and should not report every figure. Select the data that supports your overview: the highest, the lowest, the most dramatic change, and the most useful comparisons. Group similar figures together ("the three smallest sources all fell below 15%") rather than listing each one in a flat sequence.

4. Trend and change language (Band 7 range)

Verbs
rose / climbed / surged · fell / declined / dropped · fluctuated · remained stable · peaked at · bottomed out at
Nouns
a steady rise · a sharp decline · a slight fluctuation · a gradual increase · a dramatic surge
Band 7 linkers: whereas / while (for contrast within one sentence) · in contrast to · similarly / likewise · by comparison · this was followed by · this figure subsequently rose to

5. One worked example per visual type

Line graph

Data: internet usage in three countries, 2000–2020, all rising but at different rates.
Overview: "Overall, internet usage increased in all three countries over the period, although Country A overtook Country B to become the highest by 2020."

Bar chart

Data: spending by category across four age groups.
Overview: "Overall, older age groups spent proportionally more on healthcare, whereas younger groups allocated the largest share of their budget to housing."

Pie chart(s)

Data: household energy use by source, compared across two years.
Overview: "Overall, reliance on fossil fuels fell sharply between the two years shown, while the share from renewable sources rose to become the largest single category."

Table

Data: tourist arrivals by country of origin across three years.
Overview: "Overall, the number of visitors from most countries grew steadily, with one country standing out for a sudden fall in the final year."

Process diagram

No overview of "biggest trends" here — instead, state how many stages there are and whether the process is linear or cyclical, then describe each stage in sequence using the passive voice and sequencing linkers.

Overview: "Overall, the diagram illustrates a five-stage process by which raw sewage is converted into clean water for reuse, beginning with initial filtration and ending with chemical disinfection."

Map comparison

State the overall direction of change (e.g. from agricultural to residential/urban land) before detailing individual features that were added, removed, or relocated.

Overview: "Overall, the area changed from a mainly agricultural landscape in 1990 to a predominantly residential one by 2020, with a new road network replacing the original farm tracks."
Turkish L1 pattern: omitted articles Turkish has no equivalent of "a/an/the", so articles are easy to drop in English: "✗ Number of visitors increased" → "✓ The number of visitors increased." Proofread Task 1 answers specifically for missing articles before nouns referring to a specific, already-identified thing.
Turkish L1 pattern: under-use of the passive voice Turkish uses active structures more than English academic description does. In process diagrams especially, English prefers "The water is filtered" over "They filter the water" (there usually is no stated "they"). Practise converting your draft sentences from active to passive where no agent is named.

Writing Task 2 Strategy

40 minutes · 250 words minimum · a clear position, held consistently from introduction to conclusion.

1. The 4-paragraph structure

  1. Introduction — paraphrase the question, then state your thesis (your answer/position) in one clear sentence.
  2. Body paragraph 1 — one main idea, a topic sentence, an explanation, and a specific example.
  3. Body paragraph 2 — a second main idea, same pattern: topic sentence → explanation → example.
  4. Conclusion — restate your position in different words; never introduce a brand-new idea here.

2. The thesis-statement formula

"While [brief acknowledgement of the other side], this essay will argue that [your clear position], mainly because [your strongest reason]."

3. The topic-sentence method

Start every body paragraph with one sentence that states the paragraph's single main idea in your own words. Everything else in the paragraph exists only to support that sentence — if a sentence doesn't support it, cut it or move it.

4. Position consistency

Whatever you argue in the introduction must still be true in the conclusion. Examiners specifically check for this under Task Response — a position that quietly shifts loses marks even if each paragraph is well written.

5. One worked example per essay type

Opinion essay

Prompt pattern: "To what extent do you agree or disagree...?"

Thesis: "While some believe that remote work reduces productivity, this essay will argue that it in fact increases it for most employees, mainly because it removes commuting time and office distractions."

Discussion essay (both views)

Prompt pattern: "Discuss both these views and give your own opinion."

Structure: body 1 = the first view with its strongest justification; body 2 = the second view with its strongest justification; your opinion can appear in the introduction, threaded through both bodies, and restated in the conclusion.

Problem / Solution essay

Prompt pattern: "What problems does this cause, and what solutions can be suggested?"

Body 1 = problems (cause → effect); Body 2 = solutions, ideally matched one-to-one against the problems raised in Body 1.

Advantages / Disadvantages essay

Prompt pattern: "Discuss the advantages and disadvantages."

Body 1 = advantages; Body 2 = disadvantages. If the prompt also asks "do the advantages outweigh the disadvantages?", your thesis and conclusion must explicitly answer that.

Direct Question essay

Prompt pattern: two distinct questions, each needing its own answer.

Body 1 answers question 1 directly; Body 2 answers question 2 directly. Don't blend them into one vague paragraph — examiners check each question is addressed separately.
Turkish L1 pattern: translated discourse connectors Turkish essay style often produces connectors translated too literally, e.g. "In addition to this situation..." where English prefers "Moreover," / "Furthermore," / "What is more,". Keep a short list of natural English linkers and vary them rather than translating Turkish phrase-by-phrase.
Turkish L1 pattern: relative clause errors Turkish places descriptive clauses before the noun ("the [sentence] person"), which can produce English errors like "✗ The increasing pollution levels country" instead of "✓ the country where pollution levels are increasing." Check every long noun phrase for a missing relative pronoun (who/which/that/where).
